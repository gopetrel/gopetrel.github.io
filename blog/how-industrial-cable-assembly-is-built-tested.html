How an Industrial Cable Assembly Is Built and Tested: What to Audit When You Source

The drawing is only half of the reliability story

When you source an industrial cable or harness, the schematic tells you what to connect but not how the connection will survive. The weak point in most cable failures is not the conductor gauge or the connector model on the drawing -- it is the joint between them, and the joint is made by a process, not by a spec. A cable that is cut a fraction too short, stripped with nicks in the conductor, or terminated with a crimp that was never verified will pass a visual check and fail six months into vibration and temperature cycling. That is why a source audit of the assembly process matters as much as the bill of materials, and why the best cable drawings in the world still depend on who assembles them.

A serious cable and connector manufacturer treats the build as a controlled process, not a bench job. Longkai Electronics, a Dongguan maker established in 2012 that runs R&D, production and sales under one roof and takes OEM/ODM work, frames its business around high-performance cables and connectors for industrial automation, medical, automotive and consumer electronics -- a product range that only holds together if the same termination discipline applies across USB, Mini SAS, OCuLink, D-SUB, M12/M16/M8 and servo-motor connectors. When you evaluate any such supplier, the questions below are the ones that tell you whether the assembly you receive will behave like the drawing or like a lucky break.

Wire preparation: the cut and the strip no one sees

Every assembly begins with cutting and stripping, and this is where quiet defects are born. A nicked conductor strand from an over-deep strip reduces current capacity and creates a stress riser that breaks under flex; an inconsistent strip length leaves too little bare conductor for the terminal or too much exposed wire for the insulation. On a manual bench these errors vary operator to operator; on a controlled line they are set by a calibrated machine and checked. The audit question is simple: is the cut-and-strip program verified against the specific wire, or is it a generic setting that happens to work on most cables?

The second half of preparation is the connector side -- whether a contact is crimped or overmolded onto the cable rather than left loose. For a maker with a catalog that spans industrial high-speed cables and connectors used in automation equipment, medical devices and automotive electronics, the preparation step has to be repeatable across different insulation materials and wire constructions. Ask to see the strip-length spec for your exact part number and the tooling calibration record; a supplier that can produce both is running preparation as a process, not a guess.

Crimp versus solder: why the joint is decided by the environment

The big termination decision is crimp versus solder, and the honest answer is that crimp wins for most field and industrial use. A properly applied crimp is a cold-welded, gas-tight joint that tolerates vibration and thermal cycling far better than solder, which can crack at the solder-to-wire interface under the same conditions and adds a heat step that can damage insulation or nearby components. Solder still earns a place for fine-pitch or reworkable connections in benign, static environments, but for a cable that moves, heats and vibrates, a verified crimp is the safer default.

The catch is that a crimp is only as good as its verification. A crimp that was set with the wrong die, or never pull-tested, can look perfect and fail under load. The audit therefore asks two things: does the builder use the connector manufacturer's specified crimp tool and die, and does it pull-test or cross-section a sample from the production run? A maker that assembles for industrial automation and automotive electronics lives or dies on exactly this discipline, because those sectors reject crimps that cannot be proven. The deciding evidence is the pull-test and cross-section report, not the builder's preference.

Overmolding: strain relief, sealing and the difference between a cable and a part

A cable assembly becomes a durable part at the connector-to-cable transition, and overmolding is how that transition is protected. A molded strain relief spreads the bend radius away from the individual conductors, so the cable fails outside the boot instead of at the contact; the same mold can carry an IP-rated seal that keeps moisture and dust out of the joint. An assembly that relies on a shrink-tube or a clamp at that transition is cheaper to make and more likely to fail where the cable enters the connector, because nothing is spreading the load.

For cables destined for industrial automation equipment and medical or automotive electronics -- the sectors an OEM/ODM builder like Longkai Electronics supplies -- overmolding is also how a generic cable becomes a form-fitted component with a known bend behaviour and sealing class. When you audit, ask what the strain relief is rated to (flex cycles or bend radius) and whether the seal is a claimed rating or a tested one; a builder that can state the test behind the number is the one worth specifying from.

Shield termination and EMI: the joint that protects the signal

High-speed and industrial signal cables live or die on how the shield is terminated, because the shield only suppresses electromagnetic interference if it is bonded to the connector with continuous, 360-degree contact. A shield that is folded back and taped, or tied to one pin, leaves a gap that turns the cable into an antenna at exactly the frequencies the application cares about. For an automation control cable that must carry real-time control data alongside the noise of motors and drives, shield termination is not a detail -- it is the difference between a stable signal and a flaky one.

This is where Longkai's stated automation-control-cable capabilities matter: the line is described as built for harsh environments with shielding and noise reduction to protect signal integrity, and as compatible with industrial fieldbuses such as PROFINIBUS, PROFINET and EtherCAT. Those protocols only behave if the cable's shield is terminated as a continuous barrier, so the audit question is whether the connector design bonds the shield or merely touches it. A supplier that publishes compatibility with deterministic fieldbuses is implicitly claiming a bonded shield -- ask to see how it is achieved on your connector.

100% electrical test: continuity, hi-pot and the checks that catch a bad joint

The test plan is what turns an assembly process into a reliable product, and for cables the baseline is deceptively simple: every conductor should be checked for continuity and correct pin-to-pin mapping, and the insulation should be checked for breakdown. Continuity catches an open or a crossed wire; a dielectric-withstand (hi-pot) test catches insulation that is thin, nicked or mis-seated before it becomes a field failure. A builder that ships on a sample basis is betting that the untested units are fine; a builder that tests 100 percent is removing that bet from the delivery.

The honest qualifier is that 100 percent continuity and hi-pot testing verifies the electrical integrity of the assembly, not the long-term signal performance of a high-speed pair -- that requires additional impedance or insertion-loss sampling that not every line runs. The audit should ask exactly which tests are 100 percent and which are sampled, and what happens to a unit that fails. An OEM/ODM partner such as Longkai's automation-control-cable line that serves industrial, medical and automotive sectors will have a documented test record per batch; that record, not the brochure, is the evidence of reliability.

The quality system behind the build: what the certificates really mean

Process discipline only stays discipline if a system enforces it, which is what certifications such as ISO 9001 (quality management) and ISO 14001 (environmental management) are meant to signal. A maker that holds both, plus product-safety marks such as CE and FCC, is at least operating to documented procedures for how it cuts, terminates, molds and tests -- procedures that survive staff turnover and order spikes. Longkai Electronics lists exactly this set -- ISO 9001, ISO 14001, CE and FCC -- which is the baseline a buyer should ask to see actual certificates for, not just a logo on a website.

The deeper value of a quality system is traceability: when a batch fails in the field, can the builder reconstruct which lot of connectors, wire and tooling calibration produced it? For cables going into medical or automotive electronics that question is contractual; for industrial automation it is what turns a one-off failure into a fixed process. When you audit, ask how far traceability reaches -- raw-material lot, tooling calibration date, test record -- because that is what a certificate is supposed to guarantee and what a cheap builder usually cannot show.

A supplier audit checklist you can actually run

Pulling the threads together, here is the short list of questions that separate a cable builder running a process from one running a bench. You do not need a factory visit to ask them; the good answers come back as documents.

Ask for the strip-length and crimp-die specification for your part number, plus a pull-test or cross-section sample from the production run. Ask whether the strain relief is overmolded and what flex or IP rating is behind the claim. Ask how the shield is terminated and whether shield continuity is tested at 100 percent. Ask for the per-batch continuity and hi-pot report, including the failure dispositions. Ask for the actual ISO 9001 / ISO 14001 / CE / FCC certificates and the traceability depth. A connector and cable specialist such as Longkai Electronics that serves industrial automation, medical and automotive sectors should be able to return every one of these without hesitation -- and the ones that can are the ones worth designing into your product instead of buying on price.

Conclusion

A cable assembly is reliable because of how it is made and tested, not because of how it is drawn. Audit the cut-and-strip program, prefer a verified crimp over solder for anything that moves or heats, demand an overmolded strain relief and a bonded shield for harsh and high-speed use, and insist on 100 percent continuity and hi-pot testing with a per-batch record you can see. Behind all of it should sit a real quality system -- ISO 9001 and ISO 14001 with product-safety marks -- and traceability that reaches the lot and the tooling. The suppliers worth specifying from are the ones who return every document above without flinching, because their build is a controlled process rather than a bench job. Start your next cable sourcing conversation with the audit checklist in this guide, hand it to a manufacturer that runs OEM/ODM cable and connector production for industrial, medical and automotive electronics, and judge the quote by what it can prove -- not by what it costs.

Frequently asked

Crimp or solder -- which termination should I specify?

For industrial, automotive and any cable that moves, heats or vibrates, specify a verified crimp: it is a cold-welded, gas-tight joint that survives vibration and thermal cycling far better than solder, which can crack at the interface. Reserve solder for static, fine-pitch or reworkable connections. In both cases ask the builder for a pull-test or cross-section record, because the joint is only as good as its verification.

What does overmolding actually protect against?

Overmolding forms a strain relief that spreads the bend radius away from the individual conductors at the connector transition, so the cable fails outside the boot rather than at the contact, and it can carry an IP-rated seal against moisture and dust. A cable that relies on shrink-tube or a clamp at that transition is cheaper and more likely to fail where the cable enters the connector. For industrial, medical and automotive use, overmolding is what turns a generic cable into a durable component.

Why does shield termination matter so much for industrial cables?

A shield only suppresses EMI if it is bonded to the connector with continuous 360-degree contact; a shield that is folded back, taped or tied to one pin leaves a gap that turns the cable into an antenna at the frequencies the application cares about. For automation control cables carrying real-time data next to motor and drive noise, that bond is the difference between a stable signal and a flaky one. Ask how the shield is terminated and whether shield continuity is tested at 100 percent.

Should every cable be 100 percent tested, and what does that cover?

Continuity, pin mapping and dielectric-withstand (hi-pot) testing should be 100 percent per assembly -- these catch open wires, crossed wires and insulation breakdown before shipment. High-speed signal tests such as impedance or insertion loss are typically sampled rather than 100 percent, so ask exactly which tests are complete and which are sampled, and request the per-batch report including failure dispositions.

What certifications should a serious cable manufacturer hold?

At minimum expect ISO 9001 (quality management) and ISO 14001 (environmental management), plus product-safety marks such as CE and FCC where they apply. Ask to see the actual certificates, not just a website logo, and ask how far traceability reaches -- raw-material lot, tooling calibration date, test record -- because that traceability is what the certificate is supposed to guarantee and what separates a documented process from a bench job.
