What Happens After the Mold Opens: Secondary Processing, Assembly and Quality Control for Injection-Molded Parts

A buyer's guide to what turns a raw molded part into a finished component: deflashing, painting and printing, ultrasonic welding and insert molding, plating, electronic assembly, and the quality checks that catch sink marks, flash and warpage before shipment.

Ejection is not the finished part

When the press opens and the ejector pins push the part out, what lands on the conveyor is a raw molded shape, not a finished component. It still carries a gate vestige where the sprue attached, it may have a thin flash fin at the parting line, its surface is the bare resin colour, and -- unless the part is unusually simple -- it is not yet joined to anything else. The step that turns that raw shape into something a customer can assemble, sell or use is the chain of operations after molding: finishing, bonding, assembly and inspection. Buyers who only specify the molded geometry and the resin often discover late that the real cost and the real risk sit in this post-mold chain, because it is where cosmetic rejects, fit problems and field failures are actually created or caught.

A molder that treats post-mold work as a controlled process rather than an afterthought is worth more than one that quotes a low piece price on the molded blank. DAYIN, a Dongguan-headquartered injection molder established in 1989 with production bases in China and Vietnam, frames its offering explicitly as end-to-end: design, tooling, mold fabrication, mass production, post-processing and electronic assembly. That single statement -- post-processing and electronic assembly sitting in the same sentence as molding -- is the right mental model. The mold makes the shape; the operations after the mold make the product.

Deflashing and surface prep: removing what the gate left behind

The first post-mold operation is almost always cleanup. The gate where the melt entered leaves a vestige that must be clipped, ground or melted off; the parting line between the two mold halves can leave a flash fin; and ejector-pin witness marks may need smoothing. Left in place, a gate nub or a flash fin becomes a sharp edge that cuts a seal, a snag that hangs up in an automated feeder, or a visible blemish on a consumer surface. Manual trimming works for low volumes but is inconsistent operator-to-operator; cryogenic deflashing tumbles parts in frozen media that knocks off flash without touching the substrate; blast finishing cleans and textures the surface in one step.

The reason this matters to a buyer is tolerance and appearance, not just tidiness. A flash fin measured in tenths of a millimetre can still break a slip-fit between two molded halves, and a rough gate scar can fail a cosmetic sign-off even when the dimension is correct. So the deflashing method should be specified, not assumed: state the acceptable vestige height, whether the parting line must be flush, and whether the surface needs to be prepared for a subsequent coating or bond. A supplier running post-processing as a process will have a defined method per part and a check that the vestige is gone -- not a bench worker with a knife and a judgement call.

Surface finishing: painting, printing and permanent marking

Most parts need more than the natural resin colour. Spray painting adds a coloured or textured topcoat -- including soft-touch and UV-stable finishes for parts that live outdoors or in sunlight -- and it also hides the flow and gate marks that even a good mold leaves behind. Printing puts information or branding on the surface: silk-screen printing lays ink through a stencil for flat areas, while pad printing transfers ink onto curved or irregular surfaces that a flat screen cannot reach, which is why it dominates on contoured enclosures and handles. Hot-foil (hot-stamp) printing presses a metallic or coloured foil into the surface for a durable, premium mark, and laser engraving burns a permanent, wear-proof identification or logo without adding any ink or coating.

The decision is driven by what the mark must survive. A logo that only has to look good in a showroom can be printed; a serial or compliance mark that must survive handling, cleaning and years of use should be laser engraved or hot-stamped. Each method also imposes a constraint on the part: printing needs a flat or gently curved landing zone, painting needs a clean, adhesion-ready surface (which loops back to the deflashing step above), and engraving changes nothing dimensionally but is permanent. When you brief a supplier, name the method and the durability, not just "add our logo" -- the difference decides whether the mark is still legible at end of life.

Bonding and joining: ultrasonic welding versus insert molding

Many plastic products are not one shot but several, and joining them is its own discipline. Adhesive bonding is flexible and cheap to set up but slow, sensitive to surface preparation, and a cure-time bottleneck in high volume. Ultrasonic welding solves that by vibrating the joint at high frequency so the mating surfaces heat and fuse in under a second -- no adhesive, no cure, and a joint that is the same material as the part. Insert molding goes further: a metal insert (a threaded boss, a pin, a bearing seat) is placed in the mold and the plastic is shot around it, so the metal becomes permanently trapped in the plastic in a single molding step.

Each method trades off differently, and the table below is the short version a buyer should keep beside the drawing.

Joining methodBond characterCycle / setup costBest fit
Adhesive bondingChemical bond, flexible, can gap-fillSlow cure; low tooling, surface-prep sensitiveLow volume, dissimilar materials, uneven gaps
Ultrasonic weldingHomogeneous fusion of the same plasticSub-second; fixture + sonotrode toolingHigh-volume same-material housings & seams
Insert moldingMetal captured inside the plasticOne molding step; insert-handling automationPermanent threaded/metal features in a plastic part

The catch on ultrasonic welding is joint design: it needs a properly engineered energy director and a rigid enough wall, or the vibration welds the wrong place or fails to fuse. The catch on insert molding is thermal: the hot melt can loosen or shift a precision insert if the process is not controlled. A molder that offers both -- as part of a post-processing capability rather than as a subcontracted favour -- is the one who can tell you which joint your geometry actually supports.

Plating and metallization: when plastic must conduct or shine

Some functions need plastic to behave like metal. Electroplating deposits a metal layer -- typically nickel or chrome -- onto an engineered, plateable grade of plastic to give a hard, reflective, corrosion-resistant surface that reads as metal to the eye and to the touch. Vacuum metallization (sputtering or evaporation in a vacuum chamber) lays a thin, uniform metal film for decorative brightness or, more functionally, for EMI/RFI shielding on enclosures that must keep radio noise in or out. The two are not interchangeable: plating is thicker and tougher, metallization is thinner and better suited to uniform coverage on complex shapes.

The buyer's job is to separate cosmetic from functional. A shower of chrome on a trim ring is cosmetic and tolerant; a shielding layer that must hit a specific attenuation is functional and needs a measured specification, not a "it looks metallic" sign-off. Both also constrain the resin -- only certain grades accept plateable or metallizable surfaces, and the substrate must survive the process chemistry. Specifying the finish without specifying the substrate is the classic way to get parts that look right in the sample and fail in the plating tank at volume.

Electronic and mechanical assembly: from a part to a product

For a growing share of molded parts the destination is not a bin of components but a finished product, and that is where "electronic assembly" in a molder's scope stops being a footnote. A molded housing becomes a robot-vacuum shell, a pet-feeder body or a dashboard part only after a PCB is seated, a cable is routed, a lens or gasket is pressed in, and the whole thing is functionally tested. Doing molding and assembly under one roof removes a hand-off: the same plant that controls the plastic dimension also controls the fit of the board inside it, which is the interface where most "it worked as a sample, failed in production" problems actually live.

This is exactly the model DAYIN's plastic injection molding services describe -- molding, post-processing and electronic assembly as one continuous line, with the Vietnam facility running molding, assembly and packaging in a single factory as part of an established manufacturing group. For a buyer the practical implication is simple: if your part ends as a product, ask whether the molder assembles it, because a supplier who only molds will hand you a shell and a risk, while one who assembles owns the fit between the plastic and everything inside it. The assembly step is also where a functional test can catch a warped housing or a mis-seated insert before it ever reaches your line.

Quality control: reading the part, not just the drawing

Quality control is what makes the post-mold chain trustworthy, and it starts with the recognition that most molding defects are not random -- they are traceable to three variables: material shrinkage, process setting, or tooling geometry. A molder on the floor learns to read each symptom and isolate which variable drifted before a reject becomes a batch. The most common family is visual and dimensional: sink marks (localized depressions, usually opposite thick ribs or bosses, caused by uneven local shrinkage as the core cools), flash at the parting line, short shots from incomplete fill, warpage or twist after ejection, burn marks from degraded material, and flow or weld lines where melt fronts meet. Of these, sink marks are the textbook case -- the standard corrections are to raise holding pressure and hold time so the cavity stays packed while the gate freezes, add fillets or thin the offending rib so the section shrinks less, lower the melt temperature slightly, and improve the gate size.

Beyond the eye, dimensional inspection confirms the part actually matches the drawing: calipers and gauges for simple features, a coordinate-measuring machine for profiles and true-position tolerances, and a first-article report that locks the process before volume runs. Functional and life testing -- actuation cycles, seal pressure, drop or environmental exposure -- catches what a static measurement misses. A supplier running quality as a system will back it with documented procedures and recognized certifications; DAYIN lists ISO 9001 (quality management), BSCI (social compliance), GRS (recycled-content chain of custody) and IATF (automotive) among its certifications, and pairs that with in-house laboratory equipment and a stated quality-control function. The certificate is the promise; the per-batch inspection record is the evidence, and a buyer should ask for the latter, not just the former.

How to specify post-mold operations in your RFQ

Pulling the chain together, the RFQ that avoids surprises is the one that specifies the after-mold work as clearly as the molded geometry. State the acceptable gate-vestige height and parting-line finish, and whether the surface needs to be prepared for a coating or bond. Name the marking method and its durability -- printed for show, laser or hot-stamp for life. Specify the joining method and, for ultrasonic welding or insert molding, the joint or insert geometry you expect the molder to validate. For plating or metallization, separate cosmetic from functional and give a measurable target if it is functional. For assembly, describe the sub-assemblies, the test plan and the acceptance criteria, and ask whether the supplier assembles or only molds.

Then ask for the evidence: the post-processing capability list, the plating/printing/substrate compatibility, the inspection method per characteristic (100 percent versus sampled), and the actual certification documents. A manufacturer that runs molding, post-processing, electronic assembly and quality control as one integrated scope -- China and Vietnam, decades of mold-development experience and 100-plus injection machines -- should return every one of those answers without hesitation. The ones that can are the suppliers worth designing into your product instead of buying on the molded-blank price, because the part your customer receives is the one that comes out of the post-mold chain, not the one that came out of the mold.

Conclusion

A molded part is reliable and saleable because of what happens after the mold opens, not in spite of it. Deflash and prepare the surface so fits and finishes hold; choose painting, printing or engraving by what the mark must survive; pick adhesive, ultrasonic welding or insert molding by the joint your geometry supports; use plating or metallization only with a substrate and a target that match; and close the chain with assembly and inspection that read the part, not just the drawing -- catching sink marks, flash and warpage before they ship. Behind all of it should sit a real quality system with certifications you can verify and a per-batch record you can see. Start your next sourcing conversation with the post-mold checklist in this guide, hand it to a manufacturer that runs integrated molding, post-processing, electronic assembly and quality control across its China and Vietnam operations such as DAYIN, and judge the quote by what it can prove after the mold opens -- not by what the molded blank costs.

Frequently asked

What is secondary processing in injection molding?

Secondary processing is every operation performed on a part after it is ejected from the mold: deflashing and surface prep, painting, silk-screen/pad printing, hot-foil stamping and laser marking, ultrasonic welding or insert molding for joining, plating or metallization for a metal finish, and electronic/mechanical assembly. The mold creates the shape; secondary processing turns that raw shape into a finished, inspectable component.

When should I specify ultrasonic welding instead of adhesive?

Specify ultrasonic welding for high-volume joints between two parts of the same plastic, where you want a fast, adhesive-free, homogeneous fusion with no cure time -- ideal for housings and seams. Reserve adhesive for low volume, dissimilar materials, or gaps that a weld cannot close. Note that ultrasonic welding needs a properly engineered joint (an energy director and rigid walls), so the drawing must support it.

How is plating different from painting for plastic parts?

Painting adds a coloured or textured topcoat that hides mold marks and sets the colour; it is relatively thin and can wear. Plating (electroplating nickel/chrome) deposits a thicker, harder, reflective, corrosion-resistant metal layer on a plateable grade of plastic, while vacuum metallization lays a thinner uniform metal film often used for EMI/RFI shielding. Plating and metallization demand a compatible substrate and a measured target if the function is electrical rather than cosmetic.

What does injection-molding quality control actually check?

QC combines visual inspection for the common defect family -- sink marks, flash, short shots, warpage, burn marks, flow/weld lines -- with dimensional checks (calipers, gauges, CMM for profiles and true-position) and functional or life testing (actuation cycles, seal pressure, environmental exposure). Most defects trace to shrinkage, process setting or tooling geometry, so a good molder reads the symptom, isolates the variable, and corrects it before a drift becomes a rejected batch.

How do I know a supplier can handle post-mold operations and assembly?

Ask for the post-processing capability list, the plating/printing/substrate compatibility, the inspection method per characteristic (100 percent versus sampled), and the actual certification documents (for example ISO 9001, IATF, BSCI, GRS). A manufacturer that runs molding, post-processing, electronic assembly and quality control as one integrated scope -- and can return every document without hesitation -- is running these steps as a controlled process rather than a subcontracted afterthought.
