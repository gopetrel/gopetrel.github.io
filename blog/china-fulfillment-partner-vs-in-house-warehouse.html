Running Fulfilment In-House or Through a China 3PL? What the Numbers Actually Say

Most growing sellers keep fulfilment in-house far past the point where it stops making sense. A side-by-side comparison of storage, labour, shipping and cash cycle, and the break-even volume where a China-based 3PL wins.

Why fulfilment is the last thing an owner gives up

Fulfilment is operational before it is financial. The founder who started by packing orders on a kitchen table keeps packing orders long after the volume justifies a warehouse, because the switch feels like a loss of control rather than a gain of capacity. The result is a familiar pattern: order accuracy drifts, dispatch slips from 24 hours to 72, and the person who should be growing the catalogue is printing shipping labels at midnight.

The honest comparison is not "warehouse versus no warehouse" but "warehouse I run" versus "warehouse a specialist runs." The second option includes a partner whose entire business is picking, packing and dispatching on a fixed per-order basis — in China, where storage and labour are cheap enough that the storage line often disappears entirely. Dropioneer, for example, operates on a model of free Shenzhen warehousing with 24-hour dispatch and charges per order rather than per pallet-week. That structure inverts the usual economics, and it is worth understanding before you sign another lease.

What in-house fulfilment actually costs per order

Owners consistently under-count in-house fulfilment, because the visible costs are the small ones. A realistic per-order build-up for a small domestic operation looks like this: rent allocated to the storage area, the shelving and packing bench you bought once, packaging materials, the labour of picking and packing at whatever your hourly rate is, the shipping label and postage, and the software subscription that prints it. Add the two costs that never appear on an invoice — your own time, and the opportunity cost of the capital tied up in stock sitting in a warehouse that is empty for 14 hours a day.

Then there are the costs that only appear when something goes wrong. A mis-pick becomes a return, a reship and a refund; a stock-out during a promotion becomes a cancelled order and a chargeback; a peak week becomes overtime or a courier backlog. The unit economics of in-house fulfilment look fine at 50 orders a day and deteriorate sharply at 200, because labour does not scale linearly and neither does floor space.

What a China-based 3PL costs per order

A China-based 3PL charges a fulfilment fee per order — pick, pack and dispatch — plus the actual shipping cost to the destination, with storage either free or charged only above a threshold. Because the warehouse sits in the sourcing country, two other lines collapse: inbound freight from your factory becomes a short domestic move, and re-exports of returned or refurbished stock do not cross an ocean twice.

The trade is transit time. A domestic 3PL ships to a domestic customer in two days; a Shenzhen 3PL ships to the US or EU in roughly six to twelve working days on an economical line, faster on a premium one. For most direct-to-consumer brands that is acceptable, and it is explicitly the trade Dropioneer sells on: dropshipping fulfilment with automated picking and global shipping, priced so that the shipping saving offsets the extra days.

The pattern is consistent: a 3PL converts fixed costs into per-order costs and moves inventory closer to its source. Both are good for a growing brand, and both are bad for a brand with an established domestic footprint and a shipping profile that depends on two-day delivery.

The cash-cycle argument nobody puts in the spreadsheet

The line that decides most of these comparisons is not on the invoice. When stock sits in a domestic warehouse, you have already paid your supplier and you have not yet been paid by your customer — the gap is your working capital, and it grows with every SKU you add. When the same stock sits at a Shenzhen 3PL next door to the factory, the same gap exists but the inventory behind it can be leaner, because replenishment is a domestic truck rather than an ocean container.

For a brand with a long tail of slow movers this matters more than the per-order saving. The economics of holding a year of cover on a slow SKU are brutal; the economics of holding three weeks of cover next to the factory are not. This is why the decision is really about where you hold inventory, not how much — a point worth reading alongside our note on inventory positioning and peak-season scaling.

Quality control: the part that goes wrong quietly

Handing fulfilment to a third party moves the risk of a bad outbound experience off your desk, but it does not remove the risk of a bad inbound batch. The failure mode that hurts most is not a slow ship — it is a batch of defects discovered by your customers instead of by your warehouse. A 3PL picks what it receives; if the received stock is wrong, every order from that batch is wrong.

The mitigation is inspection before storage, not after dispatch. Dropioneer's model builds this in as a distinct service — pre-shipment quality control that catches bad batches before they reach a customer — which is the right sequence: inspect at the factory gate, then store, then pick. When you evaluate any fulfilment partner, ask where the inspection step sits in their flow. If the answer is "we check on arrival and flag issues," you are relying on a warehouse operative to judge a product spec. If the answer is a documented pre-shipment inspection against your sample, the risk has actually been transferred.

Where in-house fulfilment still wins

This is not an argument that every brand should outsource. In-house fulfilment remains the right answer when transit time is part of the product — same-day or next-day delivery as a headline promise, subscription boxes with complex kitting, or a catalogue of fragile items that need a specific packing method your own team has perfected. It also wins when you are small enough that the founder's own involvement in packing is a feature: early-stage brands learn more about their customers from packing their own orders than from any dashboard.

The realistic break-even sits somewhere between 100 and 300 orders a day for a cross-border brand, and higher for a domestic one. Below it, a small in-house setup is cheaper and more controllable; above it, the labour, space and capital lines compound faster than the volume does. The brands that get into trouble are the ones that never measured it — they crossed the threshold during a good quarter and discovered it during a bad one.

Conclusion

Fulfilment is a make-or-buy decision like any other, and it should be made on the same basis: compare the full cost stack including the lines that do not appear on an invoice, and identify the volume at which the answer changes. A China-based 3PL wins on storage, on labour structure, on inbound freight and on working capital, and it costs you transit days and some control over the last mile. In-house wins on speed, on bespoke handling and on the learning that comes with doing it yourself. Measure your own order volume, your real dispatch promise and your cash position, and the answer will usually be obvious — which is the opposite of how most owners actually decide.

Frequently asked

At what order volume does outsourcing fulfilment to a China 3PL make sense?

For a cross-border brand the break-even is typically between 100 and 300 orders a day. Below that, a small in-house setup is cheaper because fixed costs are still small and the founder's time is the cheapest labour available. Above it, the labour, floor space and working-capital lines grow faster than the volume, and the per-order structure of a 3PL starts winning. The exact number depends on your dispatch promise, your parcel mix and how much of your inventory is slow-moving.

How much slower is shipping from a Chinese warehouse compared with a domestic one?

Economical cross-border lines from Shenzhen typically deliver in six to twelve working days to the US and EU, with premium lines faster. Domestic fulfilment delivers in one to three days. The question is whether your customers and your marketing accept the difference — for most direct-to-consumer brands the lower shipping cost and free storage offset the extra days, but a next-day promise as a headline feature rules the model out.

Does using a 3PL mean I lose control of quality?

It means you lose control of the packing bench, not of the product. The quality risk that matters is inbound: a defective batch stored and shipped looks identical to a good one until a customer complains. The mitigation is a documented pre-shipment inspection against your sample before stock enters the warehouse, which is the sequence a competent fulfilment partner supports. Keep the spec, the sample and the inspection report; delegate the picking.

What should I check before handing my inventory to a fulfilment partner?

Ask four questions. Where does inspection happen, and against what sample? What is the real per-order fee including packaging, and what is excluded? How does stock sync with my store, and how fast? What happens to returns — are they received, inspected and restocked, or written off? The answers to those four determine whether the partner is an asset or a new set of problems.

Can I run both — in-house for fast lines and a 3PL for the rest?

Yes, and many growing brands do exactly that. A common split is domestic in-house fulfilment for a core SKU with a next-day promise and a China 3PL for the long tail and for new product tests, where the lower storage cost matters more than speed. The cost is managing two inventory positions and two integrations, which is only worth it once the volume justifies the complexity.
