Custom Plastic Toys, OEM or ODM? What to Specify Before You Ask for a Quote

A toy enquiry that arrives without a spec gets a vague quote. The resin grade, the safety standard, the MOQ logic, the packaging and the certification path — what to pin down before you contact a manufacturer.

Why vague enquiries get vague quotes

A toy enquiry that says "custom plush toys, 20 cm, please quote" produces a range of quotes so wide they are useless. One supplier assumes injection moulding, another assumes plush sewing, a third assumes vinyl; one prices a single colour, another a five-colour pad print; one assumes a polybag, another a window box. None of them is being difficult — the enquiry simply does not contain enough information to price, so each fills the gaps differently.

The fix is not a longer enquiry. It is a short, structured one. A manufacturer of custom plastic toys under OEM and ODM programmes can price an enquiry in hours when it contains the dozen or so facts below, and will quote a range — or ask clarifying questions — when it does not. The sections that follow are the facts worth settling before you send it.

OEM or ODM: the decision that shapes everything downstream

These two terms are used loosely in trade, and the distinction matters more than any other line in your enquiry. In an OEM programme you own the design: you supply drawings, a specification and usually a physical sample, and the factory manufactures to it. Tooling is built to your drawing, the product carries your brand, and you carry the design risk. In an ODM programme the factory owns the design and you adapt it: you pick from their catalogue or a base mould, then customise colour, logo, packaging and sometimes a minor feature change. Tooling already exists, so the entry cost is far lower and the lead time is far shorter — but your competitors can buy the same base product.

The practical test is how much of the product's identity is in the mould. If the shape, the snap fits and the mechanism are yours, it is OEM and you will pay for tooling. If you are choosing a stock shape and personalising it, it is ODM and you will not. A useful middle path for first orders is ODM with a custom colour and a printed logo — it proves the market before you commit to a tool. Programmes built around this route, where a brand takes an existing mould and differentiates on decoration and packaging, are standard practice at toy manufacturers that support private-label programmes.

The material question: resin grade, fillers and feel

"Plastic" is not a specification. The three resins that cover most of the market behave differently enough that the choice affects cost, safety, durability and even the feel in the hand:

ResinWhere it fitsWhat to specifyWatch out for
ABSRigid toys, building blocks, casingsGrade, colour masterbatch, gloss or matte textureNot for infants — impact strength is good, but it is rigid and can break into sharp edges
PPLiving hinges, bath toys, parts that flexHomo- or copolymer, filler contentLow surface energy — printing and glue bonds need pre-treatment
PVC (soft)Squeeze toys, inflatable components, bootsPlasticiser type — phthalate-free is mandatory in the EU and USPlasticiser migration over time; smell is a symptom of the wrong grade

Two further points decide quotes more often than the resin itself. First, colour and decoration: a single colour moulded-in is nearly free; a five-colour pad print or a full wrap transfers the part into a different cost bracket entirely. Second, any soft or textile component — a plush face on a plastic body, a fabric tag — pulls in a second process and a second supplier, which changes the assembly line and the MOQ. Specify the decoration on the drawing, not in the covering email.

Safety standards: what the destination market actually requires

This is the section that most often turns a cheap quote into an expensive one, so it belongs in the enquiry rather than after it. Every destination market has a toy safety standard, and the factory needs to know which one applies before it prices, because compliance is designed in, not inspected in:

MarketStandard familyWhat it drives
EUEN 71 (parts 1–3, plus the chemical parts 7–12)Mechanical and physical properties, flammability, and migration limits for heavy metals and plasticisers
USASTM F963 (plus CPSIA tracking labels and, where applicable, the federal phthalates ban)Use-and-abuse testing, small-parts and projectile rules, lead and phthalate limits
Both, for electrical toysEN 62115 / UL 696Battery compartments, motor heating, wiring — a separate certification path

Two practical consequences. Age grading is not marketing: a toy designed for 3+ must survive the small-parts and abuse tests for that bracket, which constrains part size and attachment strength from the tooling stage. And testing is per-material, per-colour — a new masterbatch is a new test, which is why "we will send you a new colour next month" is more expensive than it sounds. Factories that run these programmes routinely can quote against a named standard; if a supplier cannot tell you which EN 71 or ASTM F963 parts apply to your product, that is your answer about their experience.

MOQ logic: why the minimum is a function of the tool, not the product

MOQ is the question every buyer asks and the one most often misunderstood. In injection moulding the minimum order is set by the moulding cycle, not by the factory's preference: a four-cavity tool running a 45-second cycle produces a batch every three minutes, so a run of a few thousand parts is a few hours of machine time. Below that number, the setup, the colour purge and the operator's time dominate, and the per-part price climbs steeply.

The levers are all on the tooling side. Fewer cavities means a lower MOQ but a higher per-part price; a family tool that runs two or three related parts in one shot spreads the setup across the set; and a manual insert — a decal applied by hand instead of a moulded feature — removes a whole assembly step from the minimum. A supplier that quotes "MOQ 3,000, 5,000 or 10,000 depending on the mould" is telling you the truth; one that quotes a flat "MOQ 500" on a moulded product is either using an existing ODM tool or has not thought about your tool yet. First orders through an existing base mould, where the manufacturer carries the tooling, are the usual route to a low entry quantity — the same structure that makes wholesale toy supply from stock designs viable for small brands.

Packaging, labelling and the retail shelf

Packaging is not an afterthought — for a retail toy it is half the product. Three decisions have to be made before tooling, because all three affect the part:

  • The pack format. A polybag with a header card is the cheapest and the most common for promotional and wholesale programmes; a window box costs more but sells on a shelf; a blister pack suits a single small item. The format determines the printed area, and the printed area determines whether decoration is moulded-in or applied later.
  • Labelling. The EU requires a name and address on the product or packaging; the US requires a tracking label under CPSIA with the manufacturer, location, batch and date. Both are permanent marks, so they must be part of the tool design — a moulded-in mark on the part itself is standard for the tracking label.
  • Age grading and warnings. The age symbol and the associated warnings must appear on the pack, and the warning text must match the test report. Getting this wrong at the packaging stage means reprinting the whole run.

Brands selling into retail chains also face retailer-specific requirements — a specified carton size, a barcode standard, a hang-tag position — which are easier to design in at the start than to retrofit. Ask your factory whether they have produced for your target channel before; the answer shows up in the tooling rather than in the conversation.

The questions to send with your enquiry

Pulling the above together, the enquiry that gets a useful quote in one round contains: the product type and dimensions; the target market and the standard that applies; the age grading; the resin or material family; the decoration, described by process and colour count; OEM or ODM, and whether you have a drawing or a sample; your target quantity for the first order and for a steady year; the pack format and any barcode or labelling requirement; and whether the toy is electrical or contains a battery.

That is ten lines. A factory that can quote from it in a day is a factory that has done the work before; one that comes back asking "what colour?" has not. If the product is aimed at a specific channel — promotional giveaways, an Amazon listing, a retail chain — say so explicitly, because each channel has a different acceptance test, and manufacturers who serve promotional toy programmes or Amazon sellers will already know the packaging and compliance shape that channel expects.

Conclusion

Custom plastic toys are not a hard product to source; they are a hard product to specify. The cost of a toy programme is decided by a dozen choices — resin, decoration, standard, age grade, pack format, tooling route — and almost all of them are cheap to decide before the enquiry and expensive to change after it. Settle them first, in writing, and the quote you get back will be comparable across suppliers, which is the only kind of quote worth having.

Frequently asked

What is the difference between OEM and ODM for plastic toys?

In an OEM programme you own the design and the factory manufactures to your drawings and samples, so you pay for tooling and carry the design risk. In an ODM programme the factory owns a base design and you customise colour, logo and packaging on top of it, so tooling already exists and the entry cost and lead time are much lower. The trade-off is exclusivity: an ODM base product can be bought by your competitors.

How do I know which toy safety standard applies to my product?

The destination market decides it. The EU applies the EN 71 series, which covers mechanical and physical properties, flammability and chemical migration; the US applies ASTM F963 plus CPSIA requirements such as tracking labels and phthalate limits. Electrical or battery-operated toys need an additional standard — EN 62115 or UL 696. Name the standard in your enquiry so the factory can design and price for compliance from the start.

Why is the MOQ for injection-moulded toys so much higher than for plush or vinyl?

Because the minimum is set by the moulding cycle rather than by the factory's preference. A moulded part comes out of the tool on a fixed cycle — typically 30 to 60 seconds — and a run shorter than a few thousand parts is dominated by machine setup, colour purge and operator time, which makes the per-part price climb steeply. Choosing a tool with fewer cavities, a family tool that runs several parts per shot, or an existing ODM base mould are the three ways to lower the entry quantity.

Can I change the colour after the first production run?

Yes, but it is not free. A new colour means a new masterbatch, and under both EN 71 and ASTM F963 the chemical migration tests are per material and per colour, so a new colour normally requires retesting as well as a new colour match. Moulding the colour in is cheap; certifying a new one is not. If colour variants are part of the plan, agree the full colour list before the first test round.

What packaging information must be designed in from the start?

Anything permanent. The EU requires the manufacturer's name and address on the product or its packaging; the US requires a CPSIA tracking label with manufacturer, location, batch and date, usually moulded directly into the part. Age grading and its associated warning text must also match the test report. Retailers add their own carton, barcode and hang-tag specifications. All of these are far cheaper to include in the tool and artwork at the start than to add after production.
